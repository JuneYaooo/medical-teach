## Proposed response to the reviewer

We thank the reviewer for a careful and constructive report. The comments would help us to state our design, our analysis set and the limits of what these data can support much more plainly. Our point-by-point response follows. Two presentational notes apply throughout: the manuscript is not paginated in its current version, so all locations are given by section and paragraph rather than by page and line; and because the underlying data are simulated for teaching purposes, we describe observed differences rather than claiming clinical effects.

---

### Comment 1

*The authors should explain the rationale for choosing a home blood pressure monitoring research question, and should verify whether the cited references support a benefit on hard endpoints.*

**Proposed response.** We thank the reviewer for raising both points, which we address separately.

*Rationale.* Home blood pressure monitoring was chosen because it permits repeated out-of-office measurement, is widely recommended for confirming and following up elevated blood pressure, and can be delivered at scale in primary care. Our research question concerns whether a monitoring-based intervention lowers systolic blood pressure at 6 months in this setting; the measurement properties and practical delivery of home monitoring are what make that question answerable. We propose to state this rationale explicitly in the Introduction.

*Citations and hard endpoints.* We agree with the reviewer's implicit concern, and our reading of the sources is that they do **not** support a benefit on hard endpoints. The references we cite describe home measurement technique and its use for blood pressure control, and the trial we cite reported office systolic blood pressure as its primary outcome; it did not evaluate cardiovascular events or mortality. We therefore propose to remove any wording implying a reduction in cardiovascular events, and to state plainly that cardiovascular outcomes were not assessed and that 6-month systolic blood pressure is a surrogate outcome.

We are grateful for this comment: it identifies an overreach in our Introduction that we would otherwise have carried into the Discussion.

**Proposed change.**
- Introduction, paragraph 2 — add the rationale for the research question, and a sentence stating that the cited references support blood pressure lowering rather than hard-endpoint benefit.
- Discussion, Limitations, paragraph 2 — add: "Our outcome was systolic blood pressure at 6 months; cardiovascular events and mortality were not assessed, and no inference about hard-endpoint benefit can be drawn from these data."

### Comment 2

*The authors should state the primary and secondary outcomes clearly, and should avoid presenting exploratory analyses as prespecified outcomes.*

**Proposed response.** We agree, and we propose to make the outcome hierarchy explicit and traceable to the statistical analysis plan.

The primary outcome is systolic blood pressure at 6 months, compared between groups using a linear regression model adjusted for baseline systolic blood pressure, age and centre. The secondary outcome is the proportion of participants achieving the <140/90 mmHg target at 6 months; this is reported descriptively only, without a between-group test, and no inference is drawn from it. No subgroup analyses were prespecified in the analysis plan, and none were performed; consequently no exploratory analysis is presented as a prespecified outcome anywhere in the manuscript. Where any analysis departs from the plan, it is labelled post hoc at the point where it is reported.

We also propose to add a short subsection to the Results stating that no subgroup analyses were undertaken, so that the status of every analysis is unambiguous to the reader.

**Proposed change.**
- Methods, Outcomes, paragraphs 1–2 — restate primary and secondary outcomes with the model specification and the "descriptive only" designation for the secondary outcome.
- Methods, Statistical analysis, paragraph 1 — add: "No subgroup analyses were prespecified, and none were performed."
- Results, "Subgroup analyses", paragraph 1 (new subsection).

### Comment 3

*The authors should report the number of raw records, the number of participants after deduplication, the sample size of the primary analysis, and the reasons for exclusion.*

**Proposed response.** We propose to report the full flow in one place, and we regret that it was not previously given as a single sequence.

The dataset contained 303 records. Three records were identical across all 15 fields and were removed, leaving 300 unique participants, 150 in each group. Of these 300 participants, 32 were not included in the primary analysis: 31 had no 6-month blood pressure record, corresponding to participants who did not complete the 6-month follow-up, and one had an implausible age value of 999 which was set to missing under the prespecified cleaning rules, leaving that participant without a complete covariate set. The primary complete-case analysis therefore includes 268 participants, 129 in the intervention group and 139 in the control group.

Because the comment concerns denominators, we also propose to make one distinction explicit that was previously implicit: 269 participants had an available 6-month outcome, whereas 268 had both the outcome and complete covariate data. The single difference is the participant whose age was set to missing. The primary model uses 268; the secondary outcome, which requires the outcome but not complete covariate data, uses 269.

**Proposed change.**
- Results, "Participant flow and analysis set", paragraph 1 — rewrite with the successive numbers (303 → 300 → 268, with 32 not included) and the reason at each step.
- Methods, Statistical analysis, paragraph 2 — add a sentence defining the complete-case set.

### Comment 4

*The authors should explain the missing-data mechanism and how missing data were handled; unexplained mean imputation is not acceptable.*

**Proposed response.** We share the reviewer's view that imputation must never be applied silently, and we confirm that **no imputation of any kind, including mean imputation, was applied** to these data. The primary analysis is a complete-case analysis and is described as such throughout; we do not describe it as a complete intention-to-treat analysis.

*Extent and structure of missingness.* Six-month blood pressure was missing for 21 of 150 participants (14.0%) in the intervention group and 10 of 150 (6.7%) in the control group. Missingness was structural rather than scattered: the 6-month systolic and diastolic readings and the target-achievement indicator were missing together for the same participants, consistent with non-completion of the 6-month visit. These proportions are reported descriptively; no statistical test was performed on them, and we make no claim that the difference between groups is statistically significant.

*Comparison of included and non-included participants.* Participants not included in the primary analysis were slightly younger (mean 70.1 vs 72.2 years), less often male (40.6% vs 51.9%), more often in the intervention group (65.6% vs 48.1%), and had a longer mean duration of hypertension (11.8 vs 10.7 years); completed visits were similar (4.1 vs 3.8).

*Mechanism.* We propose not to assert a missing-data mechanism. The mechanism **cannot be determined from these comparisons**: the observed differences do not establish that data are missing at random, nor do they establish that they are not, and the data at hand contain no information that would allow the two to be distinguished. What we can say, and propose to say, is that the complete-case analysis may be biased and that the direction and magnitude of any such bias are unknown.

*On the HC3 analysis.* We note, in case the earlier version invited this reading, that the HC3 analysis is a sensitivity analysis for the variance specification only. It uses heteroskedasticity-consistent standard errors and does not address missing outcome data in any way.

*What is not reported.* Sensitivity analyses for missing outcome data — for example multiple imputation or worst-case scenarios — **have not been performed**, and we therefore do not report any. We propose to state this explicitly in the Limitations, so that the reader is not left to assume such an analysis was done.

**Proposed change.**
- Methods, Statistical analysis, paragraphs 3–4 — state that no imputation was used; define and justify the complete-case approach; state that the mechanism cannot be determined; state that the HC3 analysis addresses the variance specification only.
- Results, "Missing outcome data", paragraphs 1–2 (new subsection) — proportions, included/non-included comparison, and the statement that missing-data sensitivity analyses have not been performed.
- Discussion, Limitations, paragraph 1 — add: "Sensitivity analyses for missing outcome data were not performed; the complete-case analysis may therefore be biased, and the direction of any bias is unknown."

### Comment 5

*The authors should report the adjusted effect estimate with its 95% confidence interval.*

**Proposed response.** We propose to report the adjusted estimate with its confidence interval in the Abstract and in the Results, in addition to the coefficient table.

After adjustment for baseline systolic blood pressure, age and centre, the adjusted mean difference in 6-month systolic blood pressure was −5.60 mmHg (95% CI −7.67 to −3.52; P < 0.001). The corresponding estimates for the covariates were 0.97 per mmHg for baseline systolic blood pressure (SE 0.04; P < 0.001), −0.01 per year for age (SE 0.08; P = 0.908) and −0.53 for centre B versus centre A (SE 1.06; P = 0.618). For the variance-specification sensitivity analysis using HC3 standard errors, the estimate was unchanged at −5.60 mmHg (95% CI −7.69 to −3.51; P < 0.001).

We should be precise about the P values. "P < 0.001" is a reporting threshold, not an exact P value. We propose to use that threshold in the text and to retain the exact values in the coefficient table, where the group comparison is P = 2.34 × 10⁻⁷. No P value is reported as zero.

**Proposed change.**
- Abstract, Results paragraph — add the estimate and 95% CI.
- Results, "Primary outcome", paragraph 1 — add the estimate, 95% CI, P value and covariate coefficients.
- Results, "Model assumptions and sensitivity analysis for variance specification", paragraph 1 — add the HC3 estimate with its 95% CI.
- Table 2 (coefficient table) — retain exact P values, with a footnote that "P < 0.001" in the text denotes a reporting threshold.

### Comment 6

*The authors are advised to add five new subgroup analyses and to highlight the subgroup in which the result is significant.*

**Proposed response.** We thank the reviewer for this suggestion, which we read as an interest in whether the effect is consistent across patient subgroups. We propose not to add these analyses, and we would like to explain our reasoning.

First, the analysis plan prespecified no subgroup analyses and stated that no post hoc forest plot would be produced. The analyses we report follow that plan. Adding subgroup comparisons at this stage would mean defining them after the results were known, and we would not be able to assure the reviewer or the editor that they had not been chosen because of what they showed.

Second, the comment does not specify which five subgroups are intended, how they would be defined, or whether they would divide the sample into non-overlapping groups or overlapping strata. We have therefore not assumed any particular set, and we do not offer estimates of subgroup size or statistical precision, because any such figure would rest on definitions that the reviewer has not given and that we have not prespecified.

Third, presenting the subgroup with the smallest P value as a finding would constitute selective reporting. Repeating unadjusted comparisons raises the probability that at least one comparison will meet a conventional significance threshold even when no effect is present; how far it rises depends on how the subgroups are defined and on how strongly they overlap, and we therefore prefer not to attach a numerical estimate to the point.

Fourth, the appropriate method for examining whether an effect differs between subgroups is a test of treatment-by-subgroup interaction, reported for all prespecified subgroups with negative findings included, rather than a series of within-subgroup significance tests followed by a choice of the most impressive result. No interaction test was prespecified in this study, and we do not think one should be introduced retrospectively.

Finally, the dataset is simulated for teaching purposes and group assignment was generated by alternating participant identifiers rather than by randomisation, so subgroup estimates derived from it would carry no clinical meaning.

We propose instead to state explicitly in the Methods and Results that no subgroup analyses were prespecified or performed, so that readers are not left to wonder whether an analysis was done and withheld. Should the editor consider a stratified presentation essential, we would be willing to discuss descriptive summaries of the primary outcome for prespecified baseline variables, without significance testing, without ranking by P value, and explicitly labelled as exploratory and excluded from the Abstract and Conclusions.

**Proposed change.**
- Methods, Statistical analysis, paragraph 1 — add: "No subgroup analyses were prespecified, and none were performed."
- Results, "Subgroup analyses", paragraph 1 (new subsection) — add: "No subgroup analyses were prespecified, and none were performed; no post hoc subgroup comparison or forest plot is presented."
- Discussion, Limitations, paragraph 3 — add a sentence noting that effect modification was not examined.
- **No subgroup result is proposed for insertion anywhere in the manuscript.**

### Comment 7

*The authors should avoid presenting results from simulated data as evidence of clinical efficacy.*

**Proposed response.** We accept this comment fully, and we propose to make the status of the data explicit at every point where a reader might otherwise mistake it.

The data are simulated for teaching purposes. Group assignment was produced by alternating participant identifiers, not by randomisation; the participants, centres and identifiers are fictitious; and the generation parameters cannot be used to support any clinical claim. We propose to add a statement to the Abstract, to the first paragraph of the Methods and to the Limitations, and to revise wording throughout so that observed differences are described as differences in a simulated dataset rather than as evidence of efficacy. Wording such as "demonstrate", "prove" and "effective" would be removed where it refers to our own results, and the Conclusion would state what the exercise illustrates rather than what the intervention achieves.

We are particularly grateful for this comment, because it also resolves the concern raised in Comment 1: once the data are described accurately, no claim about hard endpoints can survive.

**Proposed change.**
- Abstract, last sentence — add a statement that the data are simulated and that no clinical inference is intended.
- Methods, paragraph 1 — add a statement on data generation and non-randomised group assignment.
- Discussion, Limitations, paragraph 1 and Conclusion, paragraph 1 — replace efficacy wording with descriptive wording.

### Comment 8

*The authors should provide a reproducible analysis script and should check the page and paragraph locations cited in the response letter.*

**Proposed response.** We propose to provide the analysis script and to describe exactly what it does and does not do; we also wish to be explicit about one limitation of this response.

*The script.* The script is a single Python file that takes **one input** — the raw data file, read with UTF-8-with-BOM handling — performs the cleaning steps under the rules confirmed for this project, and writes all outputs to the directory in which the script resides. It requires numpy, pandas, scipy and matplotlib; apart from those four packages it uses only the Python standard library. It creates no temporary files and does not modify the input file. The script performs the cleaning **inside** the analysis, rather than reading a pre-cleaned dataset, so that the chain from raw data to reported numbers is reproducible in a single step.

The files written are: the cleaned dataset, the cleaning log, Table 1, the primary result table, a summary report, the figure in both PNG and SVG, and a combined multi-sheet workbook. The coefficient table, the comparison of included and non-included participants, the outcome-availability comparison and the secondary-outcome proportions are written into the report and the workbook rather than as separate files. Running `python3 analysis.py` regenerates all of them and prints a console summary for comparison against a previous run. The script also directs the plotting library's cache into the project folder, so that nothing is written to the user's home directory.

*Locations.* The manuscript in its current version is not paginated, so page and line numbers cannot be given. All locations in this response are therefore specified by section and paragraph, for example "Methods, Statistical analysis, paragraph 3". We have not supplied page numbers, because any number we could give would be an estimate that would not correspond to the typeset article.

**Proposed change.**
- Supplementary Material — add the analysis script and the cleaning log.
- All locations in this response — given by section and paragraph; no page numbers.
